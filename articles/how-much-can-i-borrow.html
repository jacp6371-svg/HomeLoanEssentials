How much can I borrow?

How lenders actually work out your borrowing power, and the handful of things that move the number the most.

By Jack Perkins11 November 20266 min read

It is the first question almost everyone asks, and the honest answer is: it depends on the lender. Two banks can look at the same payslips and land tens of thousands of dollars apart. Here is how they get to their number, and what you can do about it.

The short version

Lenders work out how much income you have left each month after tax, living costs and existing debts, then test whether that surplus could cover repayments at a rate about 3% higher than the one you will actually pay. The loan size that fits inside that surplus is your borrowing power.

What lenders look at

  • Income after tax. Base salary counts in full. Overtime, bonuses, commission and rental income are often counted at a discount, commonly around 80%, because they can change.
  • Living expenses. Lenders compare what you tell them with a benchmark called the Household Expenditure Measure (HEM) and use whichever is higher. You cannot borrow more by under-reporting your spending.
  • Existing debts. Car loans, personal loans, buy now pay later and HECS-HELP all reduce what is left over.
  • Credit card limits. This one surprises people. Lenders count a monthly cost based on the limit, not the balance. A $20,000 limit you never touch still reduces your borrowing power.
  • Dependants. Each child adds to the living expense benchmark.
  • The assessment rate. APRA expects lenders to test your repayments at a buffer of about 3% above the loan's rate, so you can cope if rates rise.

Why two lenders give different answers

Every lender has its own policy on things like how much overtime it counts, how it treats self-employed income, which benchmark it uses for expenses and how it handles rental income. That is a big part of why using a broker helps: we know which lenders are more generous with your type of income.

Five ways to borrow more

  1. Cut or close credit cards you do not need, or reduce the limits.
  2. Pay off small debts like a car loan or personal loan before applying, if your savings allow.
  3. Choose the right lender for your income type, especially if you are self-employed or rely on overtime.
  4. Consider a longer loan term, which lowers the assessed repayment. You can still pay it off faster.
  5. Apply with a second income if you are buying with a partner.

Borrowing power is not the same as a comfortable budget

Being approved for a figure does not mean you should borrow all of it. We always talk about what repayment feels comfortable alongside the rest of your life, not just what the bank will allow.
