Should I refinance?

When switching lenders pays off, what it costs, and a quick checklist before you move.

By Jack Perkins11 November 20265 min read

Lenders tend to save their sharpest rates for new customers. If you have had the same loan for a few years, there is a fair chance you are paying more than you need to. Refinancing is not always the answer, though. Here is how to tell.

Signs it is worth a look

  • You have not reviewed your rate in the last year or two.
  • Your fixed rate is ending soon.
  • Your property has gone up in value, so your loan is now a smaller share of it.
  • You want to release equity for renovations or an investment.
  • You are juggling credit cards or personal loans at much higher rates.
  • Your loan is missing features you want, such as an offset account.

What it costs to switch

Refinancing is rarely free, so we always add up the costs before recommending it:

  • Discharge fee from your current lender.
  • Government fees to discharge and register the mortgage.
  • Application or valuation fees with some new lenders.
  • Break costs if you are leaving a fixed rate early.
  • Lenders Mortgage Insurance again if your new loan is above 80% of the property's value.

Some lenders offer cashback to cover these costs. A cashback is only good value if the rate and fees are competitive for the years ahead, not just on day one.

Try asking your current lender first

Sometimes a single phone call gets a lower rate without moving at all. If they will not budge, at least you know you have tested it. We are happy to tell you if staying put is the better option.

A quick checklist

  1. What is my current rate, and what are new customers being offered?
  2. How much will switching cost, all up?
  3. How long until the saving covers those costs?
  4. Am I planning to sell or make other changes soon?
  5. Does the new loan have the features I actually use?

Be careful with debt consolidation

Rolling a car loan or credit card into your home loan usually lowers your monthly repayment, but stretching a short-term debt over 30 years can mean paying more interest overall. If you consolidate, plan to pay that part off faster.
